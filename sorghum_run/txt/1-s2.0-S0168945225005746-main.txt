Sorghum bicolorABSTRACT
Copper (Cu) is required by prokaryotes and eukaryotes for fundamental life processes including photosynthesis, 
respiration and neurotransmission but excess Cu is toxic, causing severe problems ranging from weak plant 
growth to Wilson ’s disease in humans. Copper homeostasis was shown to be tightly regulated by P1B-type 
ATPase in yeast and other model organisms but the molecular mechanism by which sorghum plants maintain 
this delicate balance of Cu homeostasis remains poorly understood. We identified 31 orthologs of P1B-type metal 
transporters in Sorghum bicolor . From the identified sorghum orthologs of P1B-type ATPases, we complemented 
the yeast Δccc2 mutant with SbHMA5. We showed that SbHMA5 encodes a P1B- type Cu ATPase homologous to 
OsHMA5 and AtHMA5. The SbHMA5 transcript is detected in most sorghum tissues and induced by CuSO4 
treatment. The protein is predominantly localized in the plasma membrane and physically interacts with Cu 
chaperones SbATX1 and SbFRN3. Our results suggest that SbHMA5 is an efflux carrier involved in Cu homeo -
stasis required for sorghum growth and development.
1.Introduction
Sorghum [Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench] is a multipurpose cereal 
grown primarily for food, feed, and fuel worldwide. Due to its efficient 
C4 photosynthesis and relatively small diploid genome (~730 Mb), 
sorghum is a model for functional genomics studies of sugarcane, Mis-
canthus, maize and other C4 grasses (Paterson et al. 2009; Tadesse et al. 
2024 ). It is a drought tolerant crop that is well suited to arid and 
semi-arid regions. Its relative resilience to abiotic stresses also makes it 
an attractive target for crop improvement in suboptimal environments 
and could serve as a source of important agronomic traits for genetic 
manipulation. Despite its adaptation in edaphic conditions, sorghum ’s 
growth and development is severely impeded by the absence of critical 
nutrients and trace elements (White and Brown, 2010 ). Unfortunately, 
the molecular mechanism of regulation of macro and micronutrients in 
sorghum are not well characterized. Some heavy metals and metalloids 
such as Aluminum (Al), Cadmium (Cd), Lead (Pb), Mercury (Hg), and 
Arsenic (As) are considered non-essential to plants but can adversely 
affect crop performance and many of them pose serious risks to human 
health (Clemens and Ma, 2016; Gall et al. 2015; Rai et al. 2019 ). 
Conversely, other heavy metals such as Copper (Cu), Iron (Fe), Manganese (Mn), Cobalt (Co), and Zinc (Zn) are required by plants in 
trace amounts as they make up important components of cytochromes 
and enzymes that catalyze critical metabolic reactions including 
photosynthesis, respiration and scavenging reactive oxygen species 
(Jomova et al. 2022 ). The lack of such elements not only compromise 
plant growth and productivity but can also impact human health if diets 
are deficient in Cu or Fe leading to Menkes syndrome (Vairo et al. 2019 ) 
or anemia (Miller et al. 2013 ), respectively. Nevertheless, at higher 
concentrations, these elements become toxic and inhibit plant growth 
and development or cause health risks in humans.
The essential micronutrient Cu is required for many plant physio -
logical processes including photosynthetic electron transport, mito-
chondrial respiration, oxidative stress responses, protein and 
carbohydrate metabolism, hormone signaling, and acts as a structural 
element in regulatory proteins (Marschner and Rietbrock, 1995; Raven 
et al. 1999; Rehman and Rather, 2019 ). Furthermore, Cu ions act as 
cofactors in many enzymes such as Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase (SOD), 
cytochrome c oxidase, amino oxidase, laccase, plastocyanin , polyphenol 
oxidase , and nitrite reductases, affecting several fundamental physio -
logical processes including signaling of transcription and protein traf-
ficking, oxidative phosphorylation, and iron mobilization (Ravet and

Pilon, 2013; Rehman and Rather, 2019 ). However, at high concentra -

necrosis, stunting, leaf discoloration and inhibition of root growth 
(Chandrasekhar and Ray, 2017; Marschner and Rietbrock, 1995; Van 
Assche and Clijsters, 1990 ) due to the inhibition of enzymatic activity or 
protein function, impaired cell transport processes, and oxidative 
damage (Meharg, 1994 ). Thus, proper regulation and fine-tuning of Cu 
homeostasis is essential for plant growth and development. Similarly, Cu 
regulation is critical for prokaryotes and lower eukaryotes, and is an 
active area of molecular research due to its potential applications in 
heavy metal bioremediation (Ghasemi et al. 2025; Llorca and 
Martinez-Espinosa, 2022; Saez-Zamacona et al. 2023 ).
In Arabidopsis , two types of Cu transporters have been described, CTR 
and HMA type. The COPT1 gene and its homologues belong to the Cu 
transporter (CTR) family and encode high affinity Cu transporters that 
allow the entry of Cu into plant cells (Kampfenkel et al. 1995; Puig et al. 
2002; Sancenon et al. 2004 ). The second type of Cu transporters are 
P-type ATPases that regulate intracellular Cu homeostasis across mem-
branes. P-type ATPases transport a variety of ions including K, H, 
Na/K, H/K, Ca2, heavy metals, and phospholipids, and have been 
divided into five major groups based on sequence motifs and substrate 
specificity, namely P1B, P2A, P2B, P3A, P4-type ATPases and a sixth 
group (P5) with yet unidentified substrate (Argüello, 2003; Axelsen and 
Palmgren, 2001; Palmgren and Nissen, 2011; Rosenzweig and Argüello, 
2012 ). The P1B-type heavy-metal ATPases (HMAs) are found in all or-
ganisms from microbes to humans and transport a variety of heavy metal 
ions across membranes including those that are required for life pro-
cesses such as Cu and Zn, and those that are not required such as Cd and 
Pb (Rosenzweig, 2002; Rosenzweig and Argüello, 2012 ).
A few plant Cu transporter HMAs and their metallochaperones have 
been identified by genetic analysis (Abdel-Ghany et al. 2005; 
Andres-Colas et al. 2006; Baxter et al. 2003; Deng et al. 2013; Huang 
et al. 2016; Klaumann et al. 2011; Zheng et al. 2012 ). P1B -type ATPases 
(HMAs), are represented by eight members in Arabidopsis , denoted 
HMA1 to HMA8 (Mills et al. 2003 ). While HMA1 to HMA4 may be 
involved in Zn, Cd, and Pb homeostasis (Hirayama et al. 1999; Hussain 
et al. 2004 ), HMA5 to HMA8 are believed to play a role Cu and Ag 
homeostasis (Axelsen and Palmgren, 2001 ). Out of these, RAN1 corre -
sponds to HMA7 and delivers Cu to ethylene receptors (Chege et al. 
2020; Woeste and Kieber, 2000 ). PAA1 (HMA6) and PAA2 (HMA8) are 
required for Cu delivery into the chloroplast (Abdel-Ghany et al. 2005 ). 
HMA5 is reported to detoxify Cu in Arabidopsis roots presumably by 
extruding Cu out of the cells (Andres-Colas et al. 2006 ). In rice, OsHMA5 
is reported to be involved in Cu loading to the xylem for root-to-shoot 
translocation (Deng et al. 2013 ), while OsHMA4 is shown to prevent 
Cu accumulation in rice grain by sequestering it to the vacuole (Huang 
et al. 2016 ). Here, we describe the identification and characterization of 
SbHMA5, a transmembrane protein which appears to play a critical role 
in Cu homeostasis in Sorghum. SbHMA5 encodes a P1B-type Cu ATPase 
closely related in sequence to rice OsHMA5.
2.Materials and methods
2.1. Plant material and growth conditions
Plant material used in this study, sorghum [Sorghum bicolor (L.) 
Moench], were grown in growth chambers and greenhouses under long- 
day conditions of 16/8 h light/dark cycle at 25–28℃, 70–80 % relative 
humidity, and 150 μmol/m2 light intensity. For media growth, seeds 
were first sterilized with 70 % ethanol for 5 min, 20 % sodium hypo -
chlorite for 15 min, and gently agitated in a rocker platform and rinsed 
with sterile water. Then seeds were incubated for 3–4 days at room 
temperature on a sterile filter paper with sterile water. The germinating 
seeds were transferred to 1/2MS media (Chege et al. 2020 ) supple -
mented with 30 µM CuSO4, 3 mM MnSO4, 3 mM FeSO4, and 0.9 mM 
ZnSO4 in magenta boxes and grown in a growth chamber. Leaves from three-week old plants were collected for gene expression studies. In the 
case of seeds, regular soil grown plants were used since they cannot 
complete their life cycle in tissue culture. For metal treatments, con-
centrations were optimized for all evaluated metals beginning with 
standard concentration and slowly increasing concentrations until 
plants demonstrated a phenotype or growth inhibition.
2.2. Genome-wide analysis of plant P1B-type ATPases
The search term “metal transporter ATPase ” was used in the sorghum 
genome Sorghum bicolor v3.1.1 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/i 
nfo/Sbicolor_v3_1_1 ) to identify loci (Table S1). Sequence analysis 
using Blossom 90 amino acid similarity matrix with threshold 1 was also 
performed using Geneious Prime software (Geneious, 2024 ) against the 
eight previously identified Arabidopsis thaliana P1B-Type transporter 
genes (Table S2). Sequences that have at least 10 % similarity index 
(Table S2) with the Arabidopsis P1B-type ATPase genes were used for 
multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic study. Multiple sequence 
alignment was performed using MUSCLE in MEGA 11 (Tamura et al. 
2021 ), and phylogenetic analysis was performed in Geneious Prime 
using the neighbor joining Geneious tree builder method with 
Jukes-Cantor genetic distance model and 1000 bootstrap replicates to 
construct the neighbor joining phylogenetic tree. The tree was then 
edited with iTOL: interactive tree of life v4 (Letunic and Bork, 2019 ). 
Thirty-one closest genes from the tree were used for further analysis and 
motif prediction. Motif prediction (Table S3) was performed using 
Geneious Prime to annotate and predict function from the 31 aligned full 
length sequences.
2.3. RNA extraction, gene cloning and plasmid construction
Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) and after 
purification with ethanol, cDNA synthesis was performed by SSIV 
reverse transcriptase using an oligo (dT) primer. RT-PCR and RT-qPCR 
were performed as previously described (Zhang et al. 2014 ) using 
SbActin (Sobic.005G047100) as a control. The full-length SbHMA5 gene 
was amplified by PCR from cDNA synthesized from sorghum (BTx623 ) 
leaves. For sequencing, the SbHMA5 gene was amplified by attaching 
attB sites and the purified product was cloned to pDONOR207 . All 
primers used in this study are provided in Table S4.
2.4. Yeast complementation
The yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae ) wild type (WT) strain BY4741 
(MATa his3Δ1 leu2 Δ0 met15 Δ0 ura3 Δ0), and BY4741-derived mutant 
Δccc2( Cross-Complements Ca(2 ) phenotype of csg1, MATa his3Δ1 
leu2 Δ0 met15 Δ0 ura3 Δ0 YΔR270w: kanMX4) were obtained from Open 
Biosystems. Yeast complementation was performed as reported (Deng 
et al. 2013 ). Briefly, the full length SbHMA5 gene was cloned in the 
pYES2/CT yeast expression vector by adding HindIII and BamHI re-
striction sites to SbHMA5 and ligating this into the pYES2/CT vector, 
creating pYES2/CT- SbHMA5 . This plasmid was propagated in E. coli and 
purified with standard miniprep for yeast transformation. The new 
pYES2/CT- SbHMA5 and empty vector pYES2/CT were transformed into 
BY4741 WT and Δccc2 mutant strains using the LiAc method (Gietz and 
Schiestl, 2007 ). Transformants were cultured at 30•C overnight in 3 ml 
of SD-Ura media (6.7 g/l yeast nitrogen base, 1.92 g/l dropout mix 
without uracil) containing 2 % (w/v) glucose. Cells were washed three 
times with 10 ml sterile deionized water and the optical density at 
600 nm adjusted to 0.2 with sterile distilled water. After sequential 
10-fold dilutions, 10 µl of cell suspension of each genotype was spotted 
on Fe-limited, Fe-sufficient or Cu-sufficient media, and the plates were 
incubated at 30•C for 3 days. The Fe-limited media contained 0.17 % 
(w/v) yeast nitrogen base without CuSO4 and FeCl3 (BIO 101 Systems), 
0.2 % (w/v) dropout mix without uracil, 2 % (w/v) galactose, 1 % (w/v) 
raffinose, 50 mM MES (pH 6.1), 1 mM3-(2-pyridyl)-5, 6-bis D. 2.5. In silico protein-protein interaction analysis
In-silico protein-protein interaction was performed using the bio-
informatic prediction program STRING v11 database (Szklarczyk et al. 
2019 ) using the amino acid sequences of Sobic.006G173800. The pro-
gram was preset with settings of full string network, experiments and 
databases as active sources of interaction, and high confidence (0.7) as 
minimum interaction score.
2.6. Yeast two-hybrid analysis
For yeast two-hybrid analysis, the full-length sequence of SbHMA5 
was cloned into pGBKT7-GW as bait, while the full-length partner pro-
teins were cloned in pGADT7-GW as prey, and each pair of constructs 
were co-transformed into Y2H Gold yeast strain (Clontech). Yeast 
transformants were grown on synthetic minimal double dropout me-
dium deficient in SD/-Leu/-Trp, and interactions were assessed on triple 
dropout medium (TDO) deficient in SD/-His/-Trp/-Leu.
2.7. Bimolecular florescence complementation analysis (BiFC)
BiFC assays were conducted according to (Lu et al. 2010 ). Briefly, 
SbHMA5 cDNA was cloned into the pDONOR207 vector and subse -
quently introduced into the pSITE-nEYFP-n1 vector containing the 
N-terminal fragment of yellow fluorescent protein (YFP). The coding 
sequences of (SbATX1 and SbFRN3) were initially cloned into the pDO-
NOR207 vector and subcloned into pSITE-cEYFP-n1 vector. Each 
construct was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens stain GV2260 
by the freeze-heat shock method. Pairs of combinations were 
co-infiltrated into four weeks old Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. P19 was 
used to inhibit transgenic silencing. YFP signal was assessed after 
48–60 hrs of infiltration using a TCS SP2 AOBS confocal laser scanning 
microscope (Leica Microsystems).
2.8. Sub-cellular localization
The full length SbHMA5 sequence was cloned from BTx623 genotype 
using a gateway cloning system by removing the stop codon and 
sequenced in pDONOR207. Then, SbHMA5 was transferred to the 
destination vector pGWB505 with GFP, creating a SbHMA5-GFP fusion 
protein driven by a 35S promoter, and a 35S-GFP was used as a control. 
The final construct was transformed into A. tumefaciens GV2260. A new 
culture from a fresh colony was made and infiltrated into N. benthamiana 
leaves. P19 was used to inhibit transgenic silencing. Screening for GFP 
florescence, and imaging using a TCS SP2 AOBS confocal laser scanning 
microscope was done after 48 hrs.
3.Results
3.1. Genome wide identification of P1B-Type ATPase transporters in 
Sorghum bicolor
Based on a genome-wide search for heavy metal transporters from 
the sorghum genome, 577 sorghum genes were retrieved from Sorghum 
bicolor v3.1.1 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Sbicol 
or_v3_1_1 ) (Table S1). Protein sequence analysis showed 294 se-
quences shared at least 10 % amino acid identity with one or more of the 
eight Arabidopsis P1B-Type ATPase transporters (Table S2). Neighbor 
joining phylogenetic analysis of the 294 sorghum and eight Arabidopsis genes showed all the Arabidopsis and 31 sorghum P1B-type ATPase 
transporters grouped into one clade (highlighted green) (Fig. 1a). 
Further detailed analysis of the green highlighted clade showed that the 
11 sorghum P1B-type ATPases were grouped into the five subgroups 
described in Arabidopsis and another 20 that branched from Group IV 
but equidistant to Groups I, II, and III (Fig. 1b). Group 1 includes 
AtHMA2, AtHMA3, AtHMA4 and the three sorghum orthologs 
(Sobic.002G083000, Sobic.002G083100, and Sobic.010G250900). Group 
II includes AtHMA6, AtHMA8 and two sorghum orthologs 
(Sobic.001G484400 and Sobic.007G225000). AtHMA1 and sorghum 
ortholog Sobic.010G240500 are grouped in Group III. Group IV includes 
AtHMA5 and two sorghum orthologs (Sobic.006G173800, and 
Sobic.006G173700) (Fig. 1b). Sobic.004G07900 which was reported as 
SbHMA9 in sorghum is the next closest ortholog to AtHMA5 and 
AtHMA7 and is an outgroup in this clade (Fig. 1b). SbHMA5 is highly 
conserved across different species. All the sequence features including 
metal binding and other domains are highly conserved across Arabi -
dopsis, rice and sorghum genomes (Fig. S1). Our analysis showed that 
SbHMA5 was duplicated in the Pancoideae family following its split 
from rice. While Arabidopsis and rice contain a single copy of HMA5, 
sorghum and other Pancoideae grasses contain two copies (Fig.S2). 
Multiple sequence alignment indicated that SbHMA5 is most closely 
related to characterized Cu transporter ATPases OsHMA5 (85.7 % 
identity) and AtHMA5 (69.72 % identity) (Fig. S1).
3.2. Sobic.006G173800 (SbHMA5) encodes a P1B-type putative Cu 
transporter ATPase
The SbHMA5 gene has five exons and encodes a transmembrane 
peptide of 1082 amino acids, predicted to be a Cu transporter ATPase 
(Fig. 2a). The topology prediction of SbHMA5 with HMMTOP (Tusnady 
and Simon, 2001 ) showed eight transmembrane domains (Fig. 2b). 
Motif analysis using the Geneious Prime annotate and predict function 
showed several motifs including a long hydrophilic loop with a phos-
phorylation (DKTG), ATP-binding (SEHPL), and phosphatase (GDGIND) 
domains (Petris et al. 2002; Vonk et al. 2012 ) between transmembrane 
domains 6 and 7, two heavy metal binding motifs (GMTCAAC and 
GMACKYC) near the N-terminus, and ion transduction (CPC) motif 
within the sixth transmembrane domain (Fig. 2b, c, and Table S3.), 
which is typical of a P1B-type heavy metal ATPase (HMA). Arabidopsis 
P1B-Type genes (HMA5-HMA8) are predicted to be Cu transporters, 
while others that clustered with AtHMA1-AtHMA3 are reported to be Cd 
and Zn transporters (Cai et al. 2019; Eren and Arguello, 2004; Kim et al. 
2009; Mills et al. 2005; Mills et al. 2003 ). Together, this suggests that 
SbHMA5 is a Cu transporting ATPase.
3.3. SbHMA5 gene expression is induced by Cu and Mn treatment
Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis indicated 
that the SbHMA5 gene is expressed in most tissues including leaves, 
roots and seeds with highest expression in the leaf sheath (Fig. 3a). To 
evaluate the effect of heavy metal treatment on SbHMA5 expression, we 
grew plants in culture media supplemented with 30µM CuSO4, 3mM 
MnSO4, 0.9mM ZnSO4, and 3mM FeSO4, and analyzed SbHMA5 
expression in the leaf by RT-qPCR. We found that SbHMA5 expression 
was strongly induced by CuSO4 and to a lower extent by MnSO4, but not 
by ZnSO4 and FeSO4 (Fig. 3b). This suggests that SbHMA5 or its regu-
lator senses the elevated Cu concentration and is activated to regulate 
the excess Cu.

3.5. SbHMA5 is localized primarily in the plasma membrane
To determine the subcellular localization of SbHMA5, we fused the 
full length SbHMA5 CDS to the C-terminus of GFP in gateway vector 
pGWB505 and transformed the resulting plasmid into A. tumefaciens 
strain GV2260 using pGWB505 alone as a negative control. GV2260 was 
then infiltrated into 4-week-old N. benthamiana leaves and examined by 
confocal microscopy 48–60hrs after infiltration. Images were taken 
together with the red channel at a wavelength of 680nm for chloroplast 
auto florescence and DIC for cell outline controls. While GFP fluores -
cence was observed in the cytosol and nucleus control (Fig. 5a-d), strong 
green florescence of the plasma membrane was observed with the 
pGWB505-SbHMA5 construct (Fig. 5e-h), indicating that SbHMA5 is 
primarily localized to the plasma membrane. Some diffused GFP signals 
were also detected in the endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 5e).
3.6. SbHMA5 physically interacts with SbATX1 and SbFRN3
P1B-type heavy metal ATPases often function in a complex with 
other proteins called metallochaperones that are reported to serve as 
metal receptors and facilitate safe transport of metal ions (Arnesano 
et al. 2002; de Abreu-Neto et al. 2013 ). Therefore, we wondered what 
the SbHMA5 partners might be. Based on bioinformatic prediction using 
STRING v.11 (Szklarczyk et al. 2019 ), we identified ten proteins in the 
sorghum genome which could potentially interact with SbHMA5. This 
includes homologues of the yeast antioxidant protein ATX1 (SbATX1; 
Sb08g017782.1 ), farnesylated related protein (SbFRN3; 
Sb07g020020.1 ), heavy metal related protein (SbHMT1; 
Sb08g022260.1 ), SbCuB-like (SbCuBL; Sb10g021320.1 ) and six others 
(Fig. S3). Using AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al. 2024 ), we tested the can-
didates for a predicted interaction with SbHMA5 (Fig. 6a-e, Fig.S4). We 
tested four of the screened proteins by yeast two hybrid (Y2H) analysis 
to see which of these proteins indeed physically interact with SbHMA5. The SbHMA5 protein was cloned into pGBKT7-GW as bait and each of 
the candidate proteins were cloned into pGADT7-GW as prey, and 
pairwise interactions were evaluated in the yeast gold strain (Takara 
Bio). We found that SbHMA5 interacted with SbATX1 and SbFRN3, but 
not with SbHMT1and SbCuBL (Fig. 7a-d).
These Y2H interactions were validated in living plant cells with 
bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) using split YFP in 
which SbHMA5 was fused to the N-terminal half of YFP and the partner 
proteins were fused to the C-terminal half of YFP. Consistent with the 
Y2H result, yellow fluorescence due to the proximity of N-YFP and C- 
YFP brought about by protein-protein interactions were observed in 
SbHMA5-SbATX1 and SbHMA5-SbFRN3 pairs, but not with SbHMA5- 
SbHMT1 and SbHMA5-SbCuBL pairs (Fig. 7e-l, Fig. 8), confirming that 
SbHMA5 physically interacts with SbATX1 and SbFRN3 in yeast and 
plant cells. Taken together, our data indicates that SbHMA5 is required 
in Cu homeostasis by possibly extruding excess Cu out of the cell via the 
plasma membrane, perhaps with the help of metallochaperones SbATX1 
and SbFRN3. Six cations were evaluated for a possible binding to 
SbHMA5 using AlphaFold3 prediction and all cations including Cu, 
manganese, iron, zinc and cobalt were predicted to bind SbHMA5 but 
not at the same position (Fig. 6f). This suggests that different metals 
have specific metal binding domains.
4.Discussion
4.1. The SbHMA5 gene encodes a P1B-type Cu ATPase involved in Cu 
transport across the plasma membrane
Although Cu is required for cellular functions in all living organisms 
spanning from bacteria to humans, its cellular concentration needs to be 
tightly regulated as excess Cu is toxic to cells causing oxidative damage 
to DNA, membrane lipids, and proteins leading to disruption of cellular 
functions (Burkhead et al. 2009; Mitra et al. 2004; Pe~na et al. 1999; 
Rosenzweig and Argüello, 2012 ). In humans, while Cu deficiency due to 
mutation of ATP7A causes Menkes disease affecting vital functions 
including hematologic, neurologic, cardiovascular and immune systems 
(Guthrie et al. 2020; Kaler, 2011 ), Cu accumulation due to ATP7B mu-
tation causes Wilson disease, affecting brain, liver, kidney and other 
organs (Aggarwal and Bhatt, 2020; Patil et al. 2013 ). In Arabidopsis , 
PAA1/AtHMA6 and PAA2/AtHMA8 deliver Cu to the chloroplast and 
the paa1 paa2 double mutant is seedling (Abdel-Ghany et al. 2005 ). 
AtHMA5, on the other hand, is reported to be involved in Cu detoxifi -

Compared to the nine OsHMAs in rice and eight AtHMAs in Arabi -

HMA5 appeared to be duplicated resulting in SbHMA5 and SbHMA5L 
(Fig. S2). The HMA5 duplication on chromosome six was tandem 
(Fig. S2) and appeared to have occurred after the sorghum and rice split 
some 50–60My ago (Paterson et al. 2004; Wolfe et al. 1989 ). Aligning of 
a 20kb region around the HMA5 locus in sorghum, Setaria , Panicum , 
rice, Brachypodium, and Arabidopsis revealed that the duplication existed 
in Setaria and Panicum as well, but not in Brachypodium or Arabidopsis , 
suggesting a time frame after the sorghum and rice split, but before the 
sorghum and Panicum divergence. Whether SbHMA5L performs iden-
tical functions to SbHMA5 is unknown at this stage. It would be 
worthwhile to investigate the significance of this duplication and the 
higher number of HMAs in sorghum with respect to Cu toxicity tolerance, and plant development and biomass productivity in general.
4.2. SbHMA5 physically interacts with metallochaperones SbATX1 and 
SbFRN3
Metallochaperones are a class of metal ion receptor soluble proteins 
that bind and deliver metal ions to intracellular destinations (Capdevila 
et al. 2017; Robinson and Winge, 2010; Rosenzweig, 2002; Singh 
Sekhon, 2010 ). They are required to prevent the deleterious reactions of 
heavy metals and facilitate safe transport to different cellular compart -
ments (de Abreu-Neto et al. 2013; Rosenzweig, 2002 ). Cu(I) and Cu(II), 
for example, are highly reactive ions and form tight complexes with 
organic molecules causing damage through the generation of reactive 
oxygen species (Robinson and Winge, 2010 ) and thus, these ions are not 
freely available in the cytosol. Instead, they are bound by Cu chaperones 
and delivered to P1B-type Cu ATPases for transport to Cu enzymes and 
endomembrane sites (Gonzalez-Guerrero and Arguello, 2008; Robinson 
and Winge, 2010; Rosenzweig, 2002 ). Out of ten metallochaperone-like 
proteins predicted to interact with SbHMA5, two (ATX1 and FRN3

interact with ATP7A and ATP7B to transport Cu to intracellular desti-

plants, while Arabidopsis AtATX1 is demonstrated to interact with HMA5

Cu to these different P1B-type Cu ATPases for both intracellular delivery 
and removal from the cytosol. Thus, the interaction of SbHMA5 with 
SbATX1 could be functionally significant to Cu homeostasis, but the 
contribution of SbATX1 has not yet been evaluated in sorghum. Taken 
together, our data suggests that Cu homeostasis is critically required for 
plant growth and development in sorghum from seed germination to 
grain filling and this is primarily accomplished by a P1B-type Cu ATPase 
SbHMA5 that efflux Cu from the cytosol.
